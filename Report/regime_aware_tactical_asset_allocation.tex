\begin{abstract}
    \noindent We study whether forecasted equity and bond return regimes improve monthly allocation across equities, bonds, and cash after transaction costs. The empirical design links realised regime estimation, next-month regime forecasting, and portfolio choice. Jump-model teachers label equity and bond return environments from realised return-based features. Supervised students then forecast the next-month teacher labels from lagged macro-financial predictors. The resulting forecast probabilities are converted into regime-conditional mean--variance allocations with transaction costs and turnover penalties.

    \noindent We compare two regime representations. The \Bindep{} specification estimates separate two-state favourable and unfavourable labels for equities and bonds. The \Acoup{} specification estimates a three-state joint equity--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed regimes. Both representations distinguish the central portfolio mechanism: bonds support equity drawdowns in disinflationary episodes such as the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, while inflation-driven sell-offs such as 1994 and 2022 weaken the bond hedge. In the student stage, regularised logistic regression and random forests produce informative next-month regime probabilities. In the allocation stage, selected dynamic strategies improve on the 60/40 benchmark along distinct margins, including lower drawdowns, higher Sharpe ratios, and higher terminal wealth, depending on risk aversion and turnover control.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Balanced portfolios depend on how equity and bond returns interact across market environments. In disinflationary equity sell-offs, bond returns often rise and reduce portfolio losses. In inflation-driven sell-offs, equities and bonds can both lose value. A tactical allocation rule therefore needs information about the prevailing equity--bond environment when choosing next-month equity, bond, and cash exposures.

This paper studies monthly regime-aware allocation for a broad equity leg and a broad fixed-income leg. The empirical objects are asset-class return environments. The equity leg represents the stock component of a balanced portfolio, and the bond leg represents the fixed-income component. The allocation problem is whether information available at the end of month \(t\) supports a change in next-month exposure after transaction costs and turnover penalties.

The empirical design has three stages. First, the teacher stage
estimates realised regime labels from equity and bond returns. Second, the student stage
forecasts the next-month teacher labels from lagged macro-financial predictors. Third, the
allocation stage maps the forecast probabilities into regime-conditional return moments and
monthly equity, bond, and cash weights. The out-of-sample test asks whether the forecast
probabilities improve portfolio choice after transaction costs.

We compare two regime-label structures. The \Bindep{} approach estimates separate binary
favourable-state labels for the equity and bond legs. The \Acoup{} approach estimates one
joint equity--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed states. The independent structure
gives asset-level signals; the coupled structure gives a direct signal for the joint
diversification environment.

The rest of the paper follows the same sequence. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the return
series and predictors. Section~\ref{sec:teacher} reports the realised regime labels.
Section~\ref{sec:student} reports next-month forecast performance. Section~\ref{sec:alloc}
evaluates the allocation results. The appendices report formulas, grids, diagnostics, and
sensitivity checks.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis uses monthly excess returns for an equity leg, a bond leg, and cash.
The equity leg is the S\&P~500 total return. The bond leg is an aggregate bond-index return.
The one-month risk-free rate defines excess returns,
\[
    r^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=\text{equity return}_t-rf_t,\qquad
    r^{\mathrm{bd}}_t=\text{bond return}_t-rf_t .
\]

\paragraph{Teacher features.}
The \Bindep{} equity teacher uses daily equity exponentially weighted returns and Sortino
ratios over 21, 63, 126, and 252 trading days. The \Bindep{} bond teacher uses monthly bond
exponentially weighted returns, downside risk, Sharpe ratios, and volatility over one- to
twelve-month horizons. The \Acoup{} teacher uses monthly equity and bond Sortino, drawdown,
and trend features. Appendix~\ref{app:features} reports the formulas, horizons, and
transformations.

\paragraph{Student panel.}
The student panels are monthly and lagged. The \Bindep{} panel combines equity, Treasury,
credit, commodity, volatility, macro, sentiment, and stock--bond correlation predictors,
with causal winsorisation, rolling z-scores, and exponential smoothing. The \Acoup{} panel
uses a screened monthly predictor set for the joint equity--bond label: lagged macro-financial,
Treasury, commodity, and regime-context variables, together with current-regime indicators
and regime run length. Appendices~\ref{app:features} and~\ref{app:student-coupled} report
the predictor definitions and transformations.

\section{Teacher: regime labelling with statistical jump models}
\label{sec:teacher}

The teacher stage defines the realised regimes that the student models later forecast. A regime label must be persistent and must separate different return outcomes. Persistence measures whether a state represents a sustained environment. State-conditional returns show whether the states distinguish favourable from adverse outcomes. Crisis-window behaviour links the labels to known equity--bond episodes.


\subsection{The jump model}

A jump model assigns each observation to a finite set of states while penalising frequent state changes. For a standardised feature vector \(x_t\), state \(s_t\), and state centres \(\{c_k\}\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s_1,\dots,s_T,\,\{c_k\}}\
    \sum_{t=1}^{T} d(x_t,c_{s_t})
    +
    \lambda\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Lambda(s_{t-1},s_t),
    \label{eq:jump-generic}
\end{equation}
where \(d(\cdot,c_k)\) measures distance from state centre \(c_k\), \(\Lambda\) penalises switches, and \(\lambda\) controls persistence. Higher values of \(\lambda\) produce longer spells. Given the centres, the state sequence is obtained by dynamic programming. Given the state sequence, the centres are updated. These two steps are repeated from several initialisations.

\subsection{Two regime parameterisations}
\label{sec:two-parameterisations}

Both regime specifications use the jump-model penalty, but they define different state spaces.
Both teachers are estimated causally. The label assigned to month $t$ is produced by a
statistical jump model that, at the time of fitting, has access only to data available up to
month $t$ (data through $t$) and never to any future return. Equivalently, at point $t$ the
model is fit on the expanding history ending at $t$, so no forward-looking information enters
the teacher labels. This holds for the equity and bond teachers in the \Bindep{} approach and
for the joint teacher in the \Acoup{} approach.

\paragraph{\Bindep{}: two independent binary labels.}
The \Bindep{} teacher estimates separate two-state jump models for the equity and bond legs. For asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\), feature vector \(x^a_i\), and teacher member \(p\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s^a_1,\dots,s^a_{T_a},\,\{c^a_0,c^a_1\}}
    \sum_{i=1}^{T_a} d_p\!\left(x^a_i,c^a_{s^a_i}\right)
    +\lambda_p\sum_{i=2}^{T_a}\mathbf{1}\{s^a_i\neq s^a_{i-1}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-teacher}
\end{equation}
The equity teacher uses daily observations. The bond teacher uses monthly observations. The equity model starts after a 12-year daily training window and is updated at month ends. The bond model starts after a 144-month training window and is updated monthly. At each update, the available training features are standardised before fitting.

The teacher ensemble combines \(L^2\) and \(L^1\) jump-model members across persistence
penalties. The \(L^2\) members use squared Euclidean loss and mean centres; the \(L^1\)
members use absolute loss and medoid centres. Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-independent}
reports the persistence grids and ensemble construction.

After each fit, states are ordered by realised in-sample Sharpe ratio. Let \(g^{a,p}\) denote the higher-Sharpe state for asset \(a\) and teacher member \(p\). This state is labelled favourable. The monthly label from member \(p\) is
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{eq},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left\{
    \frac{1}{n_m}\sum_{i\in m}\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq},p}_i=g^{\mathrm{eq},p}\}
    \ge \frac{1}{2}
    \right\},
    \qquad
    S^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m=g^{\mathrm{bd},p}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-monthly-label}
\end{equation}
The equity label is the majority daily state within the month. The bond label is the monthly state itself.


\paragraph{\Acoup{}: three joint regimes with a coupling probability.}
The \Acoup{} approach represents the equity--bond environment with three joint regimes. Each
leg has a benign or adverse bit, and the admissible joint states are
\[
    \Reg=\{\,\text{calm}=(0,0),\ \ \text{normal}=(1,0),\ \ \text{stressed}=(1,1)\,\},
\]
where the pair is the equity bit and the bond bit. Calm means that both legs are benign; normal means that the equity leg is in its high-uncertainty state—positive on average but with the widest dispersion of the three regimes, spanning the disinflationary drawdowns such as the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis—while bonds remain benign and provide the hedge; and stressed means that both legs are genuinely adverse.

For a joint state $j$ with bits $(j_E,j_B)$, the per-month cost combines the two leg-specific
distances with a coupling term:
\begin{equation}
    e_t(j)=d\!\left(\zeq,\mu^{\mathrm{eq}}_{j_E}\right)+d\!\left(\zbd,\mu^{\mathrm{bd}}_{j_B}\right)
    +\eta\Big[\,\kappa_t\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in D\}+(1-\kappa_t)\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in A\}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:emission}
\end{equation}
where $A$ denotes the agree states, calm and stressed, and $D$ denotes the disagree state,
normal.
The scalar $\eta>0$ sets the strength of the coupling term, and $\kappa_t\in[0,1]$ is a learned
\emph{co-movement probability}: the model-implied probability that, given current market
conditions, the equity and bond legs occupy the same (agree) regime rather than the split
(disagree) regime. Because $\kappa_t$ multiplies the disagree state and $(1-\kappa_t)$ the agree
states in~\eqref{eq:emission}, a high $\kappa_t$ makes the agree states (calm, stressed) cheaper
and hence more likely, while a low $\kappa_t$ makes the disagree state (normal) cheaper; the
coupling term is thus a soft, data-driven prior on whether the two legs move together in
month~$t$.
Given the current state path, $\kappa_t$ is fitted by an
$L^2$-penalised logistic regression of the leg-agreement indicator
$y_t=\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=s^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\}$ on equity--bond correlation, equity
and bond trend, and equity and bond drawdown:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_t=\sigma\!\left(\beta_0+\beta^{\top}x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t\right),\qquad
    x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t=\big\{\rho^{\,\mathrm{eq,bd}}_{12,t},\ \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\
    \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\big\}.
    \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The transition penalty is applied separately to the equity and bond bits,
$\Lambda(i,j)=\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_E\neq j_E\}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_B\neq j_B\}$.
The coupling term acts as a soft bias in the state cost. It raises the cost of states that
conflict with the fitted equity--bond dependence signal and lowers the cost of states
supported by that signal.

The coupling logistic~\eqref{eq:kappa} is re-fitted at \emph{every} labelled month, with no
delay: at month~$t$ it uses only the expanding history through~$t$ (refit cadence one month),
and it is estimated \emph{jointly} with the state path inside the coordinate descent that
defines the teacher. Each iteration re-estimates the leg centres, refits the logistic of the
current leg-agreement indicator $y_t=\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=s^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\}$ on
$x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t$, and updates the path by dynamic programming under~\eqref{eq:emission}.
This also fixes the supervised/unsupervised status of the method: the jump model is
unsupervised, and although the coupling logistic is a supervised classifier, its target $y_t$
is generated by that same unsupervised model rather than by any external or forward-looking
label. The teacher stage is therefore unsupervised with respect to future returns and strictly
causal. Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-coupled} specifies the coupling logistic, its inputs, its
estimation, and its effect on the state cost in full.


\paragraph{Relationship.}
The two parameterisations define different regime objects. The \Bindep{} labels keep equity and bond regimes separate, so the final state can take any of the four favourable--unfavourable combinations. The \Acoup{} label is a single joint state. Calm corresponds to strongly favourable equity and favourable bonds; normal to uncertain equity (positive on average but spanning equity drawdowns) with favourable, hedging bonds; and stressed to unfavourable equity and unfavourable bonds. The \Bindep{} representation gives two asset-level regime signals. The \Acoup{} representation gives one regime signal for the joint equity--bond environment.

\subsection{Ensembling and voting}

The \Bindep{} teacher combines fitted paths separately for equities and bonds by majority
vote across teacher members. The \Acoup{} teacher combines 22 joint-regime members, formed
from feature families crossed with the \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) specifications. The resulting vote
shares measure member agreement. Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-independent} and
Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-coupled} report the vote formulas, transition matrices, and
member-level diagnostics.

\section{Student: forecasting the next-month regime}
\label{sec:student}

The student stage turns realised teacher labels into out-of-sample forecast probabilities.
At the end of month \(t\), the predictors \(X_t\) are observed and the target is the teacher
label in month \(t+1\). The \Bindep{} student estimates two binary favourable-state
probabilities, one for equity and one for bonds:
\begin{equation}
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}
    =
    \Pr\!\left(S^a_{t+1}=1\mid X_t\right),
    \qquad a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-student-probability}
\end{equation}

The \Acoup{} student estimates the corresponding three-class probability vector over calm,
normal, and stressed joint regimes. The student models use regularised logistic regression
and random forests; Appendix~\ref{app:student} and Appendix~\ref{app:student-coupled}
report the predictor designs, validation protocol, model grids, and full forecast
diagnostics.

are evaluated as two binary favourable-state predictions. The \Acoup{} forecast is evaluated

\section{Regime-aware allocation}
\label{sec:alloc}

The allocation stage tests whether forecast probabilities improve monthly equity--bond--cash
choice after transaction costs. At the end of month \(t\), the student forecasts are mapped
into next-month conditional return moments, and realised portfolio returns are evaluated net
of one-way transaction costs against a monthly rebalanced 60/40 benchmark.

For the \Bindep{} allocator, \(\hat p^{a}_{t+1|t}\) is the end-of-month-\(t\) probability
that asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\) is favourable in month \(t+1\). State-specific
means and variances are estimated from past returns assigned to each state and shrunk toward
pre-out-of-sample unconditional moments with weight \(n^a_{s,t}/(n^a_{s,t}+36)\). The
probability-mixture mean is
\begin{equation}
    \hat\mu^a_t
    =
    (1-\hat p^a_{t+1|t})\hat\mu^a_{0,t}
    +
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}\hat\mu^a_{1,t}.
    \label{eq:independent-allocation-mean}
\end{equation}
The variance uses the corresponding second-moment mixture, and the covariance matrix uses an
expanding stock--bond correlation estimate. Appendix~\ref{app:allocation} reports the full
probability-to-moment and realised net-return formulas.

The monthly portfolio is long-only in equity, bonds, and cash and solves
\begin{equation}
    w_t
    =
    \arg\max_{w\in\mathcal W}
    \left\{
    w^{\top}\hat\mu_t
    -
    \gamma w^{\top}\hat\Sigma_t w
    -
    \tau c \lVert w-w_{t-1}\rVert_1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:mv}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal W\) is the long-only equity--bond--cash simplex, \(\gamma\) is risk aversion,
\(c\) is the one-way transaction cost, and \(\tau\) scales turnover control.
Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-independent} reports the independent grid, selected-strategy
settings, additional benchmarks, and parameter sweeps.

The \Acoup{} allocator uses the predicted probabilities of calm, normal, and stressed joint
regimes in the same portfolio objective. Its conditional moments are formed as a probability
mixture over the three joint regimes, estimated from past returns assigned to each joint
regime using the same shrinkage, probability smoothing, and covariance construction as the
\Bindep{} allocator. The two allocators are otherwise identical: they share the long-only
equity--bond--cash universe, the $0.02$ weight grid, the mean--variance objective~\eqref{eq:mv},
the identical risk-aversion, turnover-penalty, transaction-cost, and probability-smoothing grids, and
the $10$~bps headline one-way cost, and they are evaluated on the same out-of-sample window
(1996-02 to 2024-10). They differ only in the regime representation that supplies the
conditional moments, which isolates the value of the representation itself.
Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-coupled} reports the coupled allocation details,
benchmark rules, cost sensitivity, and parameter sweeps. The headline results for both
approaches are evaluated against the 60/40 benchmark.

\subsection{Portfolio-weight behaviour}

Figure~\ref{fig:alloc-mech} reports how the selected forecasts change equity, bond, and cash
weights. Defensive allocations reduce risky exposure when adverse regimes receive higher
forecast probability. Higher-return allocations keep larger risky positions during favourable
forecasts. Low-turnover allocations reduce the size or frequency of these
changes. Full allocation diagnostics are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:alloc-independent}
and~\ref{app:alloc-coupled}.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

The sensitivity checks evaluate whether the allocation roles depend on narrow parameter
choices. Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-independent} and Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-coupled} report
the full sweeps over risk aversion, turnover penalties, transaction costs, probability
smoothing, and moment shrinkage. The main pattern is economically consistent: higher risk
aversion reduces risky exposure, smoothing and turnover penalties reduce trading, and higher
transaction costs lower net performance. The reported headline strategies are therefore
selected from stable exposure--cost trade-offs, not from a single isolated parameter setting.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The results support a benchmark-relative role for regime forecasts in monthly equity--bond
allocation. The value of the forecasts is not a single performance number. It appears through
the margin chosen by the allocator: lower drawdowns, higher risk-adjusted returns, lower
trading intensity, or higher terminal wealth.

The regime labels are statistical representations of market environments. Their validity is
therefore evaluated indirectly. A useful label must be economically separated, persistent
enough for monthly trading, predictable from lagged information, and valuable when its
forecast probability enters a portfolio rule. This sequence gives the teacher, student, and
allocation stages their economic order.

The central economic implication is that the bond leg changes role across states. In
disinflationary equity drawdowns, bonds can still hedge equity risk. In inflation-led
sell-offs, bond exposure can become part of the adverse state. This distinction explains why
asset-level regime probabilities and joint equity--bond regime probabilities are both
informative. The \Bindep{} representation gives separate signals for equity and bond
exposure; the \Acoup{} representation gives a direct signal for the diversification
environment.

The allocation evidence is strongest when read as a set of trade-offs against 60/40.
Defensive rules use regime probabilities to reduce losses in adverse states. Low-turnover
rules preserve part of that risk control with less rebalancing. High-return
rules use favourable forecasts to keep larger risky exposure. Regime information therefore
adds value when it changes exposure in the states where the static benchmark is least suited
to the equity--bond environment.
