\begin{abstract}
    \noindent We study whether forecasted equity and bond return regimes improve monthly allocation across equities, bonds, and cash after transaction costs. The empirical design estimates realised regimes, forecasts next-month regimes, and maps forecast probabilities into portfolio weights. Unsupervised jump-model teachers estimate equity and bond return environments from realised return-based features. Student models are supervised only by these teacher labels and forecast next-month regimes from lagged macro-financial predictors; the \Acoup{} student also uses current-regime indicators and regime run length. The resulting forecast probabilities are converted into regime-conditional mean--variance allocations.

    \noindent We compare two regime representations. The \Bindep{} specification estimates separate two-state favourable and unfavourable labels for equities and bonds. The \Acoup{} specification estimates a three-state joint stock--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed regimes. Both representations distinguish the central portfolio mechanism: bonds support equity drawdowns in disinflationary episodes such as the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, while inflation-driven sell-offs such as 1994 and 2022 weaken the bond hedge. In the student stage, regularised logistic regression and random forests produce informative next-month regime probabilities. In the allocation stage, selected dynamic strategies improve on the 60/40 benchmark along distinct margins, including lower drawdowns, higher Sharpe ratios, and higher terminal wealth, depending on risk aversion and turnover control.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Balanced portfolios depend on how equity and bond returns interact across market environments. In disinflationary equity sell-offs, bond returns often rise and reduce portfolio losses. In inflation-driven sell-offs, equities and bonds can both lose value. A tactical allocation rule therefore needs information about the prevailing stock--bond environment when choosing next-month equity, bond, and cash exposures.

This paper studies monthly regime-aware allocation for a broad equity leg and a broad fixed-income leg. The equity leg represents the stock component of a balanced portfolio, and the bond leg represents the fixed-income component. The empirical design has three stages. First, unsupervised jump-model teachers estimate regime labels from equity and bond returns derived features.
Second, student models forecast the next-month teacher labels from lagged macro-financial
predictors; the \Acoup{} student also uses current-regime indicators and regime run length. Third, the allocation stage maps
the forecast probabilities into regime-conditional return moments and monthly equity, bond,
and cash weights.

We compare two regime-label structures. The \Bindep{} approach estimates separate binary
favourable-state labels for the equity and bond legs. The \Acoup{} approach estimates one
joint stock--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed states. The independent structure
gives asset-level signals; the coupled structure gives a direct signal for the joint
diversification environment.

The rest of the paper follows the same sequence. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the return
series and predictors. Section~\ref{sec:teacher} reports the realised regime labels.
Section~\ref{sec:student} reports next-month forecast performance. Section~\ref{sec:alloc}
evaluates the allocation results. The appendices report formulas, grids, diagnostics, and
sensitivity checks.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis uses monthly excess returns for the equity and bond legs; cash is the zero-excess-return asset in the allocation stage.
The equity leg is the S\&P~500. The bond leg is the Bloomberg aggregate bond-index.
The one-month risk-free rate defines excess returns,
\[
    r^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=\text{equity return}_t-rf_t,\qquad
    r^{\mathrm{bd}}_t=\text{bond return}_t-rf_t .
\]

\paragraph{Teacher features.}
The \Bindep{} equity teacher uses daily equity exponentially weighted returns and Sortino
ratios over 21, 63, 126, and 252 trading days. The \Bindep{} bond teacher uses monthly bond
exponentially weighted returns, downside risk, Sharpe ratios, and volatility over one- to
twelve-month horizons. The \Acoup{} teacher uses monthly equity and bond Sortino, drawdown,
and trend features. Appendix~\ref{app:features} reports the formulas, horizons, and
transformations.

\paragraph{Student panel.}
The student panels are monthly and lagged. The \Bindep{} panel combines equity, Treasury,
credit, commodity, volatility, macro, sentiment, and stock--bond correlation predictors,
with causal winsorisation, rolling z-scores, and exponential smoothing. The \Acoup{} panel
uses lagged macro-financial, Treasury, commodity, and regime-context predictors for the joint
stock--bond label, and also includes current-regime indicators and regime run length.
Appendices~\ref{app:features} and~\ref{app:student-coupled} report the predictor definitions
and transformations.

\section{Teacher: regime labelling with statistical jump models}
\label{sec:teacher}

The teacher stage defines the realised regimes that the student models later forecast. The
jump-model teachers are unsupervised: they estimate regimes from return-based features. A regime label must be persistent and must
separate different return outcomes. Persistence measures whether a state represents a
sustained environment. State-conditional returns show whether the states distinguish
favourable from adverse outcomes. Crisis-window diagnostics compare the labels with known
stock--bond episodes.


\subsection{The jump model}

A jump model assigns each observation to a finite set of states while penalising frequent state changes. For a standardised feature vector \(x_t\), state \(s_t\), and state centres \(\{c_k\}\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s_1,\dots,s_T,\,\{c_k\}}\
    \sum_{t=1}^{T} d(x_t,c_{s_t})
    +
    \lambda\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Lambda(s_{t-1},s_t),
    \label{eq:jump-generic}
\end{equation}
where \(d(\cdot,c_k)\) measures distance from state centre \(c_k\), \(\Lambda\) penalises switches, and \(\lambda\) controls persistence. Higher values of \(\lambda\) produce longer spells. Using an iterative approach, the model uses dynamic programming to find the state sequence from the current centers, then updates the centers based on that sequence. To avoid local minima, this loop is run from several different initializations.

\subsection{Two regime parameterisations}
\label{sec:two-parameterisations}

While both regime specifications rely on the jump-model penalty, they define distinct state spaces. Both teachers are fit causally; thus, the label for month $t$ is derived strictly from observations available at or before that point.

\paragraph{Independant: two independent binary labels.}
The \Bindep{} teacher estimates separate two-state jump models for the equity and bond legs. For asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\), feature vector \(x^a_i\), and teacher member \(p\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s^a_1,\dots,s^a_{T_a},\,\{c^a_0,c^a_1\}}
    \sum_{i=1}^{T_a} d_p\!\left(x^a_i,c^a_{s^a_i}\right)
    +\lambda_p\sum_{i=2}^{T_a}\mathbf{1}\{s^a_i\neq s^a_{i-1}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-teacher}
\end{equation}
The equity teacher relies on daily observations, whereas the bond teacher uses monthly data. Both models employ an identical 12-year historical training window before starting and update at each month-end. During these updates, features are standardized using only the training sample available up to that specific date.

The teacher ensemble aggregates models across a grid of persistence penalties ($\lambda_p$) using both $L^2$ (squared Euclidean loss with mean centers) and $L^1$ (absolute loss with medoid centers) specifications. Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-independent} details the parameter grids and ensemble construction.

After each fit, hidden states are ranked by their realized in-sample Sharpe ratio, and the higher-Sharpe state ($g^{a,p}$) is labeled ``favorable.'' Because the equity teacher runs on daily data while the bond teacher runs on monthly data, their monthly target labels are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{eq},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left\{
    \frac{1}{n_m}\sum_{i\in m}\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq},p}_i=g^{\mathrm{eq},p}\}
    \ge \frac{1}{2}
    \right\},
    \qquad
    S^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m=g^{\mathrm{bd},p}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-monthly-label}
\end{equation}
Thus, the monthly equity target is a majority vote of its daily states, whereas the bond target is simply that month's state.

\paragraph{Coupled Approach: Joint Regimes with Dynamic Coupling}
The \Acoup{} approach models the stock--bond environment using three joint regimes defined by binary asset-class states:
\[
    \Reg=\{\,\text{calm}=(0,0),\ \ \text{normal}=(1,0),\ \ \text{stressed}=(1,1)\,\}.
\]
Here, the indicators denote the equity and bond states respectively ($0$ for benign, $1$ for adverse). Calm indicates both assets are benign, normal indicates adverse equities alongside benign bonds, and stressed indicates both assets are adverse. The benign-equity/adverse-bond state $(0,1)$ is explicitly excluded.

For a joint state $j = (j_E, j_B)$, the state emission cost $e_t(j)$ augments the asset-specific distances with a time-varying coupling penalty:
\begin{equation}
    e_t(j)=d\left(\zeq,\mu^{\mathrm{eq}}_{j_E}\right)+d\left(\zbd,\mu^{\mathrm{bd}}_{j_B}\right)
    +\eta\Big[\,\kappa_t\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in D\}+(1-\kappa_t)\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in A\}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:emission}
\end{equation}
where $A = \{\text{calm, stressed}\}$ and $D = \{\text{normal}\}$ partition the state space into asset agreement and disagreement. The scalar $\eta$ controls the coupling strength. This penalty acts as a soft bias within the emission cost, penalizing joint states that contradict the empirical stock--bond dependence signal while favoring those aligned with it.

The dynamic coupling parameter $\kappa_t \in [0,1]$ dictates the conditional probability of state disagreement. At each monthly iteration, $\kappa_t$ is updated via an $L^2$-penalized logistic regression. The dependent variable is the asset-agreement indicator $y_t=\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=s^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\}$, estimated conditional on a vector of macroeconomic features $x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_t=\sigma\left(\beta_0+\beta^{\top}x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t\right),\qquad
    x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t=\big\{\rho^{\,\mathrm{eq,bd}}_{12,t},\ \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\
    \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\big\}.
    \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The features comprise rolling asset-class correlation, trends, and peak-to-trough drawdowns. To guarantee causality, the regression is estimated on expanding historical samples using information up to month $t$. Finally, the sequence transition penalties scale independently across asset metrics:
\[
    \Lambda(i,j)=\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_E\neq j_E\}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_B\neq j_B\}.
\]

\paragraph{Relationship.}
The two parameterizations construct structurally distinct regime objects. While the \Bindep{} approach generates separate asset-level labels—allowing for all four permutations of favorable and unfavorable conditions—the \Acoup{} approach collapses this space into a single joint state. Specifically, calm maps to favorable equity and bonds, normal maps to unfavorable equity and favorable bonds, and stressed maps to unfavorable equity and bonds. Ultimately, the \Bindep{} representation yields two separate asset-level signals, whereas the \Acoup{} alternative produces a unified signal for the joint stock--bond environment.
\subsection{Ensembling and Voting}
The final regime labels are aggregated across a grid of persistence penalties and specifications. For the \Bindep{} approach, equity and bond paths are combined independently via a majority vote across all constituent models. For the \Acoup{} approach, the ensemble aggregates 22 joint-regime specifications formed by crossing distinct feature families with the $L^1$ and $L^2$ loss configurations. In both settings, the resulting vote shares quantify model consensus. Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-independent} and Appendix~\ref{app:teacher-coupled} detail the explicit voting formulas, transition matrices, and model-level diagnostics.

\section{Student: forecasting the next-month regime}
\label{sec:student}

The student stage turns realised teacher labels into out-of-sample forecast probabilities.
At the end of month \(t\), the predictors \(X_t\) are observed and the target is the teacher
label in month \(t+1\). The \Bindep{} student estimates two binary favourable-state
probabilities, one for equity and one for bonds:
\begin{equation}
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}
    =
    \Pr\!\left(S^a_{t+1}=1\mid X_t\right),
    \qquad a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-student-probability}
\end{equation}

The \Acoup{} student estimates the corresponding three-class probability vector over calm,
normal, and stressed joint regimes. The student models use regularised logistic regression
and random forests; Appendix~\ref{app:student} and Appendix~\ref{app:student-coupled}
report the predictor designs, validation protocol, model grids, and full forecast
diagnostics.

\section{Regime-aware allocation}
\label{sec:alloc}

The allocation stage assesses whether student forecast probabilities enhance a monthly equity-bond-cash portfolio after accounting for transaction costs. At the end of each month $t$, these forecasts are mapped into regime-conditional return moments, which then serve as inputs for a mean-variance optimization to determine target portfolio weights. We evaluate the realized returns net of one-way transaction costs, comparing the performance against a monthly rebalanced 60/40 and additional benchmarks.

For the \Bindep{} allocator, \(\hat p^{a}_{t+1|t}\) is the end-of-month-\(t\) probability
that asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\) is favourable in month \(t+1\). State-specific
means and variances are estimated from past returns assigned to each state and shrunk toward
pre-out-of-sample unconditional moments with weight \(n^a_{s,t}/(n^a_{s,t}+36)\). The
probability-mixture mean is
\begin{equation}
    \hat\mu^a_t
    =
    (1-\hat p^a_{t+1|t})\hat\mu^a_{0,t}
    +
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}\hat\mu^a_{1,t}.
    \label{eq:independent-allocation-mean}
\end{equation}
The variance uses the corresponding second-moment mixture. The covariance matrix uses an
expanding stock--bond correlation estimate shrunk toward the pre-prediction stock--bond
correlation. Appendix~\ref{app:allocation} reports the full probability-to-moment and
realised net-return formulas.

For the \Bindep{} allocator, the monthly portfolio is long-only in equity, bonds, and cash and solves
\begin{equation}
    w_t
    =
    \arg\max_{w\in\mathcal W}
    \left\{
    w^{\top}\hat\mu_t
    -
    \gamma w^{\top}\hat\Sigma_t w
    -
    \tau c \lVert w-w_{t-1}\rVert_1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:mv}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal W\) is the long-only equity--bond--cash simplex, \(\gamma\) is risk aversion,
\(c\) is the one-way transaction cost, and \(\tau\) scales turnover control.
Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-independent} reports the independent grid, selected-strategy
settings, additional benchmarks, and parameter sweeps.

The \Acoup{} allocator uses the predicted probabilities of calm, normal, and stressed joint
regimes in the same portfolio objective. Its conditional return moments are estimated from
past returns assigned to the corresponding joint regimes, using either the most likely regime
or a probability mixture, and its covariance matrix is estimated with an exponentially
weighted stock--bond covariance with a 12-month half-life. Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-coupled}
reports the coupled allocation details, benchmark rules, cost sensitivity, and parameter
sweeps. The headline results for both approaches are evaluated against the 60/40 benchmark. The comparison is benchmark-relative
within each approach; Appendices~\ref{app:alloc-independent} and~\ref{app:alloc-coupled}
report the approach-specific allocation grids, covariance estimators, and turnover-penalty
conventions.

\subsection{Portfolio-weight behaviour}

Figure~\ref{fig:alloc-mech} illustrates the empirical allocation mechanics and the resulting trade-offs between position smoothing and turnover. As visible in the structural profiles across weight plots, the optimization behavior splits along strategic objectives:

The high-return rules frequently trigger discrete, binary switches between asset classes, hitting corner solutions where the portfolio shifts entirely into a single dominant asset class. This behavior occurs because the optimization targets maximum conditional returns, causing aggressive portfolio rebalancing and sharp spikes in rolling turnover whenever a regime shift is predicted.

Conversely, the defensive and low-turnover configurations exhibit highly continuous, smooth weight trajectories. Rather than abruptly abandoning an asset class, these strategies scale exposures gradually in response to shifting forecast probabilities.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

Appendices~\ref{app:alloc-independent} and~\ref{app:alloc-coupled} report sensitivity to

and turnover penalties reduce trading, and higher transaction costs lower net performance.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether regimes estimated from stock and bond return behaviour can be
forecast one month ahead and used in allocation. The answer is yes, but in a
benchmark-relative sense. Regime forecasts create useful portfolio choices along different margins: drawdown
control, Sharpe improvement, turnover reduction, and terminal wealth.

The first contribution is the construction of economically meaningful teacher labels. The
\Bindep{} labels separate favourable and unfavourable environments for each asset class:
favourable equity and bond states have positive Sharpe ratios, while unfavourable states have
negative Sharpe ratios. The \Acoup{} labels separate the stock--bond environment directly.
Calm and normal regimes describe periods in which at least one risky leg remains useful,
whereas stressed regimes identify months in which both equity and bond returns are adverse.
This matters because the allocation problem is not only whether equities are attractive, but
whether bonds still diversify equity risk.

The crisis-window evidence gives the clearest economic interpretation of the labels.
During the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, the euro-sovereign episode, and the
Covid crash, bond exposure remained favourable and helped the balanced portfolio. During the
1994 bond shock and the 2022 inflation episode, bond exposure became part of the adverse
environment. The regime models therefore recover the distinction that motivated the project:
some equity sell-offs preserve the stock--bond hedge, while inflation-led sell-offs can
damage both legs at the same time.

The second contribution is the separation between regime identification and regime
forecasting. The teacher stage uses realised information to define the states. The student
stage then asks whether those states are predictable from lagged macro-financial and
regime-context variables. The out-of-sample diagnostics show that this step is feasible:
the \Bindep{} equity and bond labels are forecast with strong binary performance, and the
\Acoup{} labels retain useful information despite the harder three-class joint-regime task.
This makes the exercise more demanding than an ex-post regime interpretation.

The allocation results show where the forecasts have economic value. Defensive rules use
adverse-regime probabilities to reduce drawdowns relative to 60/40. Low-turnover and balanced
rules retain part of this risk control while limiting rebalancing. High-return rules use
favourable forecasts to keep larger risky exposure and improve terminal wealth. The evidence
therefore supports regime forecasts as allocation inputs, not as stand-alone market-timing
signals.
