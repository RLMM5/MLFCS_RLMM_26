\begin{abstract}
    \noindent We study whether forecasted equity and bond return regimes improve monthly allocation across equities, bonds, and cash after transaction costs. The empirical design links realised regime estimation, next-month regime forecasting, and portfolio choice. Jump-model teachers label equity and bond return environments from realised return-based features. Supervised students then forecast the next-month teacher labels from lagged macro-financial predictors. The resulting forecast probabilities are converted into regime-conditional mean--variance allocations with transaction costs and turnover penalties.

    \noindent We compare two regime representations. The \Bindep{} specification estimates separate two-state favourable and unfavourable labels for equities and bonds. The \Acoup{} specification estimates a three-state joint stock--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed regimes. Both representations distinguish the central portfolio mechanism: bonds support equity drawdowns in disinflationary episodes such as the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, while inflation-driven sell-offs such as 1994 and 2022 weaken the bond hedge. In the student stage, regularised logistic regression and random forests produce informative next-month regime probabilities. In the allocation stage, selected dynamic strategies improve on the 60/40 benchmark along distinct margins, including lower drawdowns, higher Sharpe ratios, and higher terminal wealth, depending on risk aversion and turnover control.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Balanced portfolios depend on how equity and bond returns interact across market environments. In disinflationary equity sell-offs, bond returns often rise and reduce portfolio losses. In inflation-driven sell-offs, equities and bonds can both lose value. A tactical allocation rule therefore needs information about the prevailing stock--bond environment when choosing next-month equity, bond, and cash exposures.

This paper studies monthly regime-aware allocation for a broad equity leg and a broad fixed-income leg. The empirical objects are asset-class return environments. The equity leg represents the stock component of a balanced portfolio, and the bond leg represents the fixed-income component. The allocation problem is whether information available at the end of month \(t\) supports a change in next-month exposure after transaction costs and turnover penalties.

The empirical design has three stages. The teacher stage forms realised regime labels from equity and bond returns. The student stage forecasts the next-month teacher label from lagged macro-financial predictors. The allocation stage converts forecast probabilities into regime-conditional return moments and monthly portfolio weights. A regime label summarises a persistent return environment. The allocation test evaluates whether forecast probabilities add economic value once they are mapped into implementable portfolio rules.

We compare two regime-label structures. The \Bindep{} approach estimates separate binary labels for the equity and bond legs. Each leg is classified as favourable or unfavourable. The \Acoup{} approach estimates a joint stock--bond label with calm, normal, and stressed states. The independent structure keeps the two asset-level signals separate. The coupled structure gives a direct label for the joint diversification environment.

The empirical analysis is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the return series, teacher features, and student predictors. Section~\ref{sec:teacher} defines the jump-model teachers and reports the label diagnostics. Section~\ref{sec:student} reports next-month prediction results. Section~\ref{sec:alloc} evaluates the regime-aware allocation rules against the 60/40 benchmark. The appendices report formulas, estimation details, diagnostic figures, and parameter-sensitivity checks.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
The empirical analysis uses monthly excess returns for a broad equity leg and a broad bond leg. The equity leg is based on the S\&P~500 total return. The bond leg is based on an aggregate bond-index return. A one-month risk-free rate defines excess returns,
\[
    r^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=\text{equity return}_t-rf_t,\qquad
    r^{\mathrm{bd}}_t=\text{bond return}_t-rf_t .
\]
The bond leg contains both duration exposure and spread-sensitive components, so the student predictor panel includes Treasury-market variables and corporate-credit variables.

The teacher and student stages use different information sets. The teacher stage uses realised return information from the equity and bond legs to form regime labels. The student stage uses lagged macro-financial predictors to forecast those labels out of sample. This separation defines realised labels before the supervised forecasting problem is estimated.

\paragraph{Teacher features.}
The teacher features describe recent return performance, downside risk, volatility, and risk-adjusted performance. In the \Bindep{} specification, the equity teacher is estimated from daily equity excess returns using exponentially weighted returns and Sortino ratios over 21, 63, 126, and 252 trading days. The bond teacher is estimated from monthly bond excess returns using exponentially weighted returns, downside-risk measures, rolling Sharpe ratios, and volatility over horizons from one to twelve months. Table~\ref{tab:teacher-features} summarizes the teacher feature blocks. Appendix~\ref{app:features} reports the formulas.

\paragraph{Student panel.}
The \Bindep{} student panel is monthly and lagged. It combines equity factor returns, industry and size--value portfolio returns, Treasury returns, credit-fund returns, commodity returns, financial-condition indexes, interest-rate variables, inflation, volatility, macro activity, sentiment, and stock--bond correlation measures. The base panel contains 134 predictors. Each predictor is winsorised with past information, transformed into a causal rolling z-score, and smoothed with an exponentially weighted average. Appendix~\ref{app:features} gives the data sources, predictor definitions, and transformation formulas. The student models use these lagged predictors to forecast the next-month teacher label.

The \Acoup{} student uses a separate monthly predictor construction with causal screening and current-regime context. Its predictors are described with the coupled results.

\section{Teacher: regime labelling with statistical jump models}
\label{sec:teacher}

The teacher stage defines the realised regimes that the student models later forecast. A regime label must be persistent and must separate different return outcomes. Persistence measures whether a state represents a sustained environment. State-conditional returns show whether the states distinguish favourable from adverse outcomes. Crisis-window behaviour links the labels to known stock--bond episodes.


\subsection{The jump model}

A jump model assigns each observation to a finite set of states while penalising frequent state changes. For a standardised feature vector \(x_t\), state \(s_t\), and state centres \(\{c_k\}\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s_1,\dots,s_T,\,\{c_k\}}\
    \sum_{t=1}^{T} d(x_t,c_{s_t})
    +
    \lambda\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Lambda(s_{t-1},s_t),
    \label{eq:jump-generic}
\end{equation}
where \(d(\cdot,c_k)\) measures distance from state centre \(c_k\), \(\Lambda\) penalises switches, and \(\lambda\) controls persistence. Higher values of \(\lambda\) produce longer spells. Given the centres, the state sequence is obtained by dynamic programming. Given the state sequence, the centres are updated. These two steps are repeated from several initialisations.

\subsection{Two regime parameterisations}
\label{sec:two-parameterisations}

Both regime specifications use the jump-model penalty, but they define different state spaces.

\paragraph{\Bindep{}: two independent binary labels.}
The \Bindep{} teacher estimates separate two-state jump models for the equity and bond legs. For asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\), feature vector \(x^a_i\), and teacher member \(p\), the state sequence solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{s^a_1,\dots,s^a_{T_a},\,\{c^a_0,c^a_1\}}
    \sum_{i=1}^{T_a} d_p\!\left(x^a_i,c^a_{s^a_i}\right)
    +\lambda_p\sum_{i=2}^{T_a}\mathbf{1}\{s^a_i\neq s^a_{i-1}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-teacher}
\end{equation}
The equity teacher uses daily observations. The bond teacher uses monthly observations. The equity model starts after a 12-year daily training window and is updated at month ends. The bond model starts after a 144-month training window and is updated monthly. At each update, the available training features are standardised before fitting.

The teacher ensemble combines two distance choices with a grid of jump penalties. The \(L^2\) specification uses squared Euclidean loss and mean centres. The \(L^1\) specification uses absolute loss and medoid centres. The equity grid is $\lambda^{\mathrm{eq}}_p\in\{2,4,8,16,32,64,128\}$ and the bond grid is
$\lambda^{\mathrm{bd}}_p\in
    \{0,10^{-5},10^{-4},0.001,0.00390625,0.0078125,0.015625,
    0.03125,0.0625,0.125,$ $0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}.$

After each fit, states are ordered by realised in-sample Sharpe ratio. Let \(g^{a,p}\) denote the higher-Sharpe state for asset \(a\) and teacher member \(p\). This state is labelled favourable. The monthly label from member \(p\) is
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{eq},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left\{
    \frac{1}{n_m}\sum_{i\in m}\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq},p}_i=g^{\mathrm{eq},p}\}
    \ge \frac{1}{2}
    \right\},
    \qquad
    S^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{bd},p}_m=g^{\mathrm{bd},p}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-monthly-label}
\end{equation}
The equity label is the majority daily state within the month. The bond label is the monthly state itself.


\paragraph{\Acoup{}: three joint regimes with a coupling probability.}
The \Acoup{} approach represents the stock--bond environment with three joint regimes. Each
leg has a benign or adverse bit, and the admissible joint states are
\[
    \Reg=\{\,\text{calm}=(0,0),\ \ \text{normal}=(1,0),\ \ \text{stressed}=(1,1)\,\},
\]
where the pair is the equity bit and the bond bit. Calm means that both legs are benign,
normal means that equity is adverse while bonds remain benign, and stressed means that both
legs are adverse. The benign-equity/adverse-bond state is excluded in this specification.

For a joint state $j$ with bits $(j_E,j_B)$, the per-month cost combines the two leg-specific
distances with a coupling term:
\begin{equation}
    e_t(j)=d\!\left(\zeq,\mu^{\mathrm{eq}}_{j_E}\right)+d\!\left(\zbd,\mu^{\mathrm{bd}}_{j_B}\right)
    +\eta\Big[\,\kappa_t\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in D\}+(1\kappa_t)\,\mathbf{1}\{j\in A\}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:emission}
\end{equation}
where $A$ denotes the agree states, calm and stressed, and $D$ denotes the disagree state,
normal. The scalar $\eta$ controls the strength of the coupling term. The variable
$\kappa_t\in[0,1]$ is a learned coupling probability: low values favour the disagree state, while
high values favour the agree states. Given the current state path, $\kappa_t$ is fitted by an
$L^2$-penalised logistic regression of the leg-agreement indicator
$y_t=\mathbf{1}\{s^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=s^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\}$ on equity--bond correlation, equity
and bond trend, and equity and bond drawdown:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_t=\sigma\!\left(\beta_0+\beta^{\top}x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t\right),\qquad
    x^{\mathrm{mkt}}_t=\big\{\rho^{\,\mathrm{eq,bd}}_{12,t},\ \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\
    \mathrm{trend}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{eq}}_t,\ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{bd}}_t\big\}.
    \label{eq:wt}
\end{equation}
The transition penalty is applied separately to the equity and bond bits,
$\Lambda(i,j)=\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_E\neq j_E\}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\{i_B\neq j_B\}$.
The coupling term acts as a soft bias in the state cost. It raises the cost of states that
conflict with the fitted stock--bond dependence signal and lowers the cost of states
supported by that signal.

\paragraph{Relationship.}
The two parameterisations define different regime objects. The \Bindep{} labels keep equity and bond regimes separate, so the final state can take any of the four favourable--unfavourable combinations. The \Acoup{} label is a single joint state. Calm corresponds to favourable equity and favourable bonds, normal to unfavourable equity and favourable bonds, and stressed to unfavourable equity and unfavourable bonds. The \Bindep{} representation gives two asset-level regime signals. The \Acoup{} representation gives one regime signal for the joint stock--bond environment.

\subsection{Ensembling and voting}

The \Bindep{} teacher combines fitted paths separately for each asset. For asset \(a\), the member vote share and final majority label are
\begin{equation}
    \pi^a_m
    =
    \frac{1}{|\Path_a|}
    \sum_{p\in\Path_a}S^{a,p}_m,
    \qquad
    S^a_m
    =
    \mathbf{1}\{\pi^a_m\ge 1/2\}.
    \label{eq:independent-vote}
\end{equation}
The final independent teacher label is \(S^a_m\). The vote share \(\pi^a_m\) measures agreement across teacher members and gives one favourable-state vote share for equities and one for bonds.

The \Acoup{} teacher uses 22 members, formed from eleven feature families crossed with the \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) specifications. The final joint label is the equal-weight majority vote across these members. The coupled vote shares measure member agreement over the calm, normal, and stressed states.

\paragraph{Persistence.}
The labels are persistent enough for a monthly allocation rule with transaction costs. A
label that switches too often would mainly increase turnover. Through November 2024, the
\Bindep{} labels switch 51 times for equities and 41 times for bonds. The favourable states
last \(12.8\) months on average for equities and \(13.8\) months for bonds. In the \Acoup{}
sample, the coupled ensemble records 58 regime switches across 442 months. Normal is the
most persistent joint regime, with an average duration of \(12.2\) months. Stressed is
shorter-lived, with an average duration of \(2.2\) months. The transition matrices are
reported in Appendices~\ref{app:teacher-independent} and~\ref{app:teacher-coupled}.

\paragraph{Crisis windows.}
Table~\ref{tab:crisis} compares the labels across historical stress windows. The allocation
question is whether bonds diversify equity losses or lose value together with equities.
During the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, the bond leg remains mostly
favourable in the \Bindep{} specification, and the \Acoup{} specification assigns most
months to normal. These windows correspond to equity weakness with bond support. In the
1994 bond shock and the 2022 inflation sell-off, the bond label is mostly unfavourable in
the \Bindep{} specification, and the \Acoup{} specification assigns a large share of months
to stressed. These windows correspond to joint stock--bond losses. Forecasting this
distinction is central for allocation because it separates equity drawdowns in which bonds
remain useful from environments in which both risky legs should be reduced.

\section{Student: forecasting the next-month regime}
\label{sec:student}

The student stage estimates the conditional distribution of next-month regimes using information available at the end of the current month. For month \(t\), the target is the teacher label at \(t+1\), and the predictors are the lagged macro-financial variables described in Section~\ref{sec:data}. The \Bindep{} student estimates separate binary forecasts for the equity and bond favourable states. The \Acoup{} student estimates a three-class forecast for the joint calm, normal, and stressed regimes.

For the independent student, let \(S^a_{t+1}\in\{0,1\}\) denote the next-month majority-vote label for asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\), and let \(X_t\) denote the predictor vector observed at month \(t\). The student forecast is
\begin{equation}
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}
    =
    \Pr\!\left(S^a_{t+1}=1\mid X_t\right),
    \qquad a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}.
    \label{eq:independent-student-probability}
\end{equation}
This probability is the forecasted likelihood that asset \(a\) is in its favourable regime next month. The allocation rule uses it to form regime-conditional return moments.

The \Bindep{} student estimates the two favourable-state probabilities with regularised
logistic regression and random forests. The comparison uses two predictor designs: smoothed
z-scores alone, and z-scores combined with smoothed z-scores. The validation design is
walk-forward: each out-of-sample forecast is based on a training window, a subsequent
validation window used for hyperparameter selection, and a refit on the combined
training--validation sample before the next-month forecast is recorded. The main text reports
the forecast objects and headline out-of-sample results; Appendix~\ref{app:student} gives
the model grids and validation protocol, and Appendix~\ref{app:student-regime-prediction-diagnostics}
reports the full independent prediction diagnostics.

The \Acoup{} student estimates the same forecasting problem for the joint three-state label.
It uses multinomial logistic regression and random forests to produce a probability vector
over calm, normal, and stressed regimes. The coupled forecast therefore gives one predicted
distribution for the next-month stock--bond environment, while the independent forecast gives
two asset-level favourable-state probabilities. Appendix~\ref{app:student-coupled} reports
the coupled student specification and its forecast diagnostics.

\paragraph{Forecast quality.}
Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports out-of-sample forecast performance for the student models.
The purpose of this step is not only classification accuracy. The allocation rule uses
forecast probabilities to form conditional means and variances, so the forecasts must contain
information about next-month regimes and must assign useful probability mass across states.
In the \Bindep{} panel, logistic regression is the strongest headline model for both
asset-level labels. It has the highest AUC for the equity and bond favourable-state
forecasts, and it also gives the best headline Brier and log-loss values. The full
independent predictor-design comparison is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:student-oos-prediction-summary}.

The \Acoup{} panel evaluates the joint three-class forecast. Random forests give the better
probabilistic scores, with lower Brier and log-loss values, while logistic regression gives
the higher balanced accuracy. This difference matters because the coupled allocator uses the
full probability vector over calm, normal, and stressed regimes. The allocation results in
Section~\ref{sec:alloc} therefore test whether these statistical forecast differences
translate into different portfolio outcomes.

\section{Regime-aware allocation}
\label{sec:alloc}

The allocation stage tests whether forecasted regimes improve monthly portfolio choice after
transaction costs. At the end of month \(t\), the student forecasts determine
regime-conditional estimates of next-month return moments. The portfolio rule then chooses
equity, bond, and cash weights. Realised returns are measured net of one-way transaction
costs and compared with a monthly rebalanced 60/40 benchmark.

For the independent allocator, let \(\hat p^{a}_{t+1|t}\) denote the probability, observed at
the end of month \(t\), that asset \(a\in\{\mathrm{eq},\mathrm{bd}\}\) is in its favourable
regime in month \(t+1\). For each asset and state \(s\in\{0,1\}\), the state-conditional mean
and variance are estimated from past returns assigned to that state. These estimates use only
information available before month \(t+1\). To reduce sampling error in small state samples,
the state moments are shrunk toward pre-out-of-sample unconditional moments with weight
\[
    \frac{n^a_{s,t}}{n^a_{s,t}+36},
\]
where \(n^a_{s,t}\) is the number of past observations for asset \(a\) in state \(s\). The
full probability-to-moment construction is given in Appendix~\ref{app:allocation}, and the
sensitivity to the shrinkage constant is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:allocation-parameter-sensitivity}.

The probability-mixture mean for asset \(a\) is
\begin{equation}
    \hat\mu^a_t
    =
    (1-\hat p^a_{t+1|t})\hat\mu^a_{0,t}
    +
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}\hat\mu^a_{1,t}.
    \label{eq:independent-allocation-mean}
\end{equation}
The corresponding variance uses the second moment of the two-state mixture,
\begin{equation}
    (\hat\sigma^a_t)^2
    =
    (1-\hat p^a_{t+1|t})
    \left[(\hat\sigma^a_{0,t})^2+(\hat\mu^a_{0,t})^2\right]
    +
    \hat p^a_{t+1|t}
    \left[(\hat\sigma^a_{1,t})^2+(\hat\mu^a_{1,t})^2\right]
    -
    (\hat\mu^a_t)^2 .
    \label{eq:independent-allocation-variance}
\end{equation}
The covariance matrix combines these conditional variances with an expanding stock--bond
correlation estimate.

The independent strategies use the long-only set
\[
    \mathcal W
    =
    \left\{
    (w^{\mathrm{eq}},w^{\mathrm{bd}}):
    w^{\mathrm{eq}}\ge0,\ 
    w^{\mathrm{bd}}\ge0,\ 
    w^{\mathrm{eq}}+w^{\mathrm{bd}}\le1
    \right\},
\]
with cash weight \(1-w^{\mathrm{eq}}-w^{\mathrm{bd}}\). The monthly portfolio is
\begin{equation}
    w_t
    =
    \arg\max_{w\in\mathcal W}
    \left\{
    w^{\top}\hat\mu_t
    -
    \gamma\,w^{\top}\hat\Sigma_t w
    -
    \tau c\,\lVert w-w_{t-1}\rVert_1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:mv}
\end{equation}
where \(\gamma\) is risk aversion, \(c\) is the one-way transaction cost, and \(\tau\) scales
the turnover penalty. The realised net portfolio return is
\[
    r^{p}_{t+1}
    =
    w^{\mathrm{eq}}_t r^{\mathrm{eq}}_{t+1}
    +
    w^{\mathrm{bd}}_t r^{\mathrm{bd}}_{t+1}
    -
    c\,\lVert w_t-w_{t-1}\rVert_1 .
\]
The independent grid varies the student model, predictor design, risk aversion, turnover
penalty, transaction cost, and probability-smoothing half-life. Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-independent}
reports the grid, selected-strategy settings, additional benchmarks, and parameter sweeps.

The \Acoup{} allocator applies the same portfolio objective to the joint-regime forecast. Its
inputs are the predicted probabilities of calm, normal, and stressed regimes, and its moments
are estimated from past returns assigned to those joint regimes. The coupled specification
uses either the most likely joint regime or the probability mixture
\[
    \hat\mu_t
    =
    \sum_k p_{k,t}\hat\mu_{k,t}.
\]
The coupled portfolios are long-only in equity, bonds, and cash, rebalance monthly, and use
the 10 bps one-way transaction-cost.
Appendix~\ref{app:alloc-coupled} reports the coupled allocation details, benchmark rules,
cost sensitivity, and parameter sweeps. The headline results for both approaches are
evaluated against the 60/40 benchmark.

\subsection{Portfolio-weight behaviour}

The selected allocations differ in how forecast probabilities change exposure to equity,
bonds, and cash. Defensive allocations reduce equity and bond exposure when adverse regimes
receive higher forecast probability. This lowers volatility and drawdowns, but it also limits
participation during strong equity markets. Higher-return allocations keep larger risky
positions during favourable forecasts and therefore compound more in sustained favourable
periods. The intermediate allocations sit between these cases by reducing exposure less
aggressively and trading less frequently.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

The sensitivity checks test whether the selected allocation roles depend on narrow parameter
choices. The main pattern is stable across the independent and coupled specifications:
higher risk aversion reduces risky exposure, probability smoothing reduces trading, turnover
penalties lower rebalancing, and higher transaction costs reduce net performance. The full
independent and coupled sweeps are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:alloc-independent}
and~\ref{app:alloc-coupled}. The independent selection protocol is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:independent-model-selection-protocol}, which separates fixed design
choices, grid-selected choices, and diagnostic checks.

In the \Bindep{} results, risk aversion and probability smoothing control different parts of
the allocation behaviour. For the defensive allocation, raising risk aversion from
\(\gamma=0.25\) to \(\gamma=5\) raises the Sharpe ratio from \(0.64\) to \(0.84\), reduces
maximum drawdown from \(-19.3\%\) to \(-11.6\%\), and lowers annual turnover from
\(113.7\%\) to \(54.8\%\). Probability smoothing mainly reduces trading: for the
low-turnover allocation, increasing the smoothing half-life from \(0\) to \(6\) months lowers
annual turnover from \(196.9\%\) to \(38.9\%\), with the Sharpe ratio falling from \(0.74\)
to \(0.62\). Table~\ref{tab:allocation-cost-turnover-sensitivity} reports the independent
transaction-cost and turnover-penalty sensitivity; at the 10 bps headline cost, the best
reported Sharpe ratios are \(0.74\), \(0.81\), and \(0.73\) for the high-return, defensive,
and low-turnover allocations.

In the \Acoup{} results, the parameter sweeps show the same economic pattern. For the
random-forest mean--variance allocation, increasing risk aversion from \(\gamma=0.25\) to
\(\gamma=5\) raises the Sharpe ratio from \(0.37\) to \(0.60\) and lowers annual turnover
from \(114\%\) to \(58\%\). The logistic mean--variance allocation is flatter over the same
risk-aversion range, with Sharpe ratios near \(0.60\). A small positive turnover penalty gives
the strongest net Sharpe ratio, while large penalties reduce performance by limiting the
portfolio response to regime forecasts. Transaction costs reduce the probability-mixture
mean--variance Sharpe ratio from \(0.62\) at 5 bps to \(0.57\) at 25 bps, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:c-cost}.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The empirical question is whether regime forecasts improve the timing of equity, bond, and
cash exposure after transaction costs. The results give a conditional answer. Regime
probabilities improve portfolio outcomes when the allocation rule uses them to reduce risky
exposure in adverse states, maintain exposure in favourable states, and control the trading
needed to move between those positions.

The main economic result is that the stock--bond hedge is state dependent. In the dot-com
bust and the global financial crisis, equity weakness coincides with positive bond
performance. In 1994 and 2022, bond losses occur together with equity losses. A monthly
allocation rule that treats these environments alike misses the central difference between a
diversifying drawdown and a joint stock--bond sell-off.

The two regime representations use different information about this difference. The
\Bindep{} specification gives separate equity and bond favourable-state probabilities, which
lets the allocator adjust each leg separately. The \Acoup{} specification gives a joint
probability distribution over calm, normal, and stressed regimes, which makes the
diversification state explicit. The empirical results show gains under both representations,
but along different margins: the independent allocation is strongest for terminal wealth in
the high-return role, while the coupled allocation is strongest for drawdown control in the
defensive role.

The forecasting stage is necessary because the allocation is made before the next return is
observed. The student models produce informative out-of-sample regime probabilities from
lagged macro-financial predictors. The predictor evidence is consistent with the target
definitions: equity regimes depend more on equity cross-section, industry, and style
information, while bond regimes depend more on Treasury, credit, and cross-asset conditions.

The portfolio results qualify the interpretation. Regime information has no single value
independent of the allocation rule. Its effect depends on risk aversion, turnover penalties,
transaction costs, probability smoothing, and moment shrinkage. The contribution of the
framework is to connect realised regime labels, forecast probabilities, and net allocation
performance in one monthly stock--bond decision. In that setting, forecasted regimes improve
portfolio choice when they identify when bonds hedge equity risk, when both risky legs should
be reduced, and when favourable states justify higher risky exposure.
